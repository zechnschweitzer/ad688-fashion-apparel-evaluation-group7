Opportunity Scorecard Methodology

Our filtered dataset has only 19 postings. Usable salary data exists for 4 of them. Usable experience data exists for 9 (see Market Baseline). That is too little consistent data to train and validate a regression or classification model without drawing conclusions from a handful of records, so instead of fitting a statistical model to data that cannot support one, our team built the Opportunity Score: a weighted, rule-based scoring model that ranks every posting in the sample using the three fields with strong coverage across all 19 records. This is a deliberate modeling choice, not a workaround. A regression or classifier trained on 19 rows, 4 of which have salary, would overfit and produce confident-looking numbers that mean nothing; a transparent, equal-weighted scoring model is the model that actually fits data this size. Salary and remote status are kept as separate context instead of being folded into the score, since folding in a field only 4 postings report would let those 4 records drive the ranking for the other 15.

Each of the 19 postings gets an Opportunity Score built from three component scores.

  • Skill fit score (0 to 4): how many of our team’s four self-rated strongest skills (Data Analysis, Microsoft Excel, Stakeholder Management, Project Management, from the Skill Gap Analysis) show up in that posting’s listed skills.
  • Accessibility score (0 to 3): a tiered score based on minimum years of experience required. A 0 year requirement scores highest. The score drops as the requirement climbs.
  • Employer signal score (1 to 5): the number of postings from that same employer in our sample. It is a proxy for how actively that company is hiring for this kind of role.

Salary and work arrangement were left out of the score. Only 4 of the 19 postings have usable data for each. Scoring on fields that sparse would let a handful of postings with reported data drive the ranking for the other 15. Market Baseline reports those two fields separately instead.

The three components carry equal weight. Our team had no outcome data, like actual application or offer results, to fit weights against instead. The employer signal score is not capped the same way the other two are. It is a raw count, and it currently ranges from 1 to 5 across our employers. That means an employer with many repeat postings can move the total by more per point than a skill fit or accessibility point can. Read the Opportunity Score alongside its three components, not as a single number on its own. That is why all three columns stay visible in the table below.

Opportunity Scorecard

Code 
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("data/processed/apparel_analyst_clean.csv")

team_strong_skills = ["Data Analysis", "Microsoft Excel", "Stakeholder Management", "Project Management"]

def skill_fit_score(skills_text):
    if pd.isna(skills_text):
        return 0
    matches = 0
    for strong in team_strong_skills:
        if strong.lower() in str(skills_text).lower():
            matches += 1
    return matches

df["SKILL_FIT_SCORE"] = df["SKILLS_LIST"].apply(skill_fit_score)

def accessibility_score(years):
    if years == 0:
        return 3
    elif years <= 2:
        return 2
    elif years <= 5:
        return 1
    else:
        return 0

df["ACCESSIBILITY_SCORE"] = df["MIN_YEARS_EXPERIENCE"].apply(accessibility_score)

employer_counts = df["COMPANY_NAME"].value_counts()
df["EMPLOYER_SIGNAL_SCORE"] = df["COMPANY_NAME"].map(employer_counts)

df["OPPORTUNITY_SCORE"] = df["SKILL_FIT_SCORE"] + df["ACCESSIBILITY_SCORE"] + df["EMPLOYER_SIGNAL_SCORE"]

scorecard = df[["TITLE_CLEAN", "COMPANY_NAME", "SKILL_FIT_SCORE", "ACCESSIBILITY_SCORE", "EMPLOYER_SIGNAL_SCORE", "OPPORTUNITY_SCORE"]].sort_values("OPPORTUNITY_SCORE", ascending=False).reset_index(drop=True)

Scoring Model Diagnostics

A rule-based scoring model still needs to be checked, not just described. Since we have no real hiring outcomes to validate the Opportunity Score against, we instead check its internal consistency: how much each component actually drives the final score, and whether any single component is quietly dominating the ranking.

Code 
component_correlations = df[[
    "SKILL_FIT_SCORE",
    "ACCESSIBILITY_SCORE",
    "EMPLOYER_SIGNAL_SCORE",
    "OPPORTUNITY_SCORE"
]].corr(method="spearman")["OPPORTUNITY_SCORE"].drop("OPPORTUNITY_SCORE")

component_correlations = component_correlations.rename("Correlation with Opportunity Score").round(2)

component_range = df[[
    "SKILL_FIT_SCORE",
    "ACCESSIBILITY_SCORE",
    "EMPLOYER_SIGNAL_SCORE"
]].agg(["min", "max", "mean"]).T.round(2)

component_range.columns = ["Min", "Max", "Mean"]
component_range.index = ["Skill Fit", "Accessibility", "Employer Signal"]

component_correlations.index = ["Skill Fit", "Accessibility", "Employer Signal"]

diagnostics = component_range.join(component_correlations)

Employer signal score has the widest range (1 to 5, uncapped) of the three components, and the table above shows it also carries by far the strongest rank correlation with the final Opportunity Score (0.87). Skill fit correlates more moderately (0.42), and accessibility correlates essentially not at all, slightly negative at -0.11. That confirms what the methodology note above flags: employer signal has more influence on the ranking than skill fit or accessibility, because it is a raw count rather than a capped 0-to-3 or 0-to-4 score, and it also means accessibility is currently closer to a tiebreaker than a real driver of the ranking. That is a property of this specific weighting choice, not a bug, but it is exactly the kind of thing a scoring model needs to disclose rather than hide behind a single combined number. A future version of this model could cap employer signal the same way the other two components are capped, or weight the three components unevenly on purpose, once there is outcome data to justify that choice.

What the Scores Show

Vuori appears four times in the top six rows. This is a direct result of how often the company posts similar roles in our dataset, not a fluke of the scoring method. That repetition is what the employer signal score is meant to capture: a company hiring repeatedly for related work is a more realistic target than one with a single listing. The Sr. Manager, Ecommerce Analytics role at Vuori sits at the top of the list because it combines that hiring pattern with a moderate skill overlap and a low experience bar.

Adidas’ Manager, Digital Marketing Analytics role is the strongest non-Vuori result, driven mainly by skill fit rather than employer signal, since Adidas appears less often in the filtered postings. A role can score well either because it closely matches skills our team already has, or because the employer is clearly hiring at volume. Those are different types of opportunity. A few roles near the bottom of the list, like the TJX and Foot Locker postings, score lower mainly on skill fit, which lines up with the gaps found in the skill-gap analysis rather than a lack of accessibility or employer activity.

Evaluating the Score

Vuori and TJX, the two employers the scorecard ranks highest, are also the two employers with the most postings in Market Baseline. Low skill fit scores line up with the gaps found in the Skill Gap Analysis: Power BI, SQL, and Python. The scorecard has not been checked against real hiring outcomes. Use it to prioritize search and skill building, not to predict who gets an offer.

What This Means for Our Team

The scorecard points us toward applying early and often to Vuori and TJX postings. Both the raw counts and the scorecard agree these employers are hiring repeatedly for adjacent roles. Adidas postings are a good check on skill fit. They reward the skills our team already rates itself strong in. The lower scoring postings show which skills would open up the most additional opportunities if built up. That is where Final Recommendations picks up.
